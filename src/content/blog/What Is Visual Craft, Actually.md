I'm coming up on a year into this job, and I've been reflecting on my growth. Problem decomposition, systems thinking, product strategy — I can feel the improvement there. I can easily point to it.

Visual craft is harder to articulate. After talking through it with my mentor, I think I have a better idea of what it is and how to see it over time. 

## Visual craft is both science and art

There's a foundational layer — hierarchy, typography, color, spacing — the stuff you can actually study. And then there's the other half: taste. The part that only comes from repetition, from looking at a lot of things, from testing and being wrong over and over.

Many junior designers think good craft is about making something look cool or beautiful. But in the context of UX, visual craft isn't about making something look cool. My mentor said "if you want to make something look cool, go be an artist." 

Craft, in this context, is about making something _feel_ the way it was supposed to feel — and making sure it actually works.

I keep coming back to the cooking competition, _Culinary Class Wars_. One of the judges never scored dishes on his own taste. He scored them on whether the dish delivered what it was trying to deliver. A curry that tastes punchy and loud but was supposed to feel like home — like nostalgia — has failed, even if it tastes incredible. Regardless of whether that is because the chef lacked intention or the diner didn't understand it. The bottom line is it didn't tell the story the chef intended it to. 

That's the test. Not "is this beautiful," but "did this become what it meant to become."

## Every design decision needs intention

My mentor has a belief he's said to me more than once: every visual decision needs a rationale. If you can defend it, you're probably doing good design. I believe this too.

But sometimes, rationales conflict. Two defensible choices can point in opposite directions, and "I have a reason" stops being useful as a tiebreaker.

When that happens, user experience wins. Which decision is best for the user? 

Everything below that is taste and testing. You show the work. You ship it and watch what happens. You ask what you learned. And slowly, that feedback becomes the taste you're building — not a rule you follow, but an instinct you're accumulating one rep at a time.

## Some tangible ways to improve craft

A few things have actually moved the needle for me:

- Iterate more. Do  "a dozen more iterations" exercise, even when you think you are already done, and react to each. Think of the rationales for why some stand out more than others.
- Asking other designers how something _feels_ — not what they'd change, just how it lands. Jamming on visuals specifically, not the whole problem.
- Remembering that visual design is present in every project, even the unglamorous ones, and treating that as an opportunity instead of an afterthought.
- **Ask yourself "why" on every visual decision, even the small ones. Especially the small ones.**

## How do you measure your own craft?

Here's the thing that finally made this feel less like a black box: keep a visual record of your work over time.

I went back and looked at stuff I made two years ago. It looks bad now. Genuinely bad. But a year ago, I couldn't have told you _why_ it looked bad, or what I'd have done differently. I didn't have the opinion yet.

Now I do. I can look at old work and articulate exactly what's off — the hierarchy, the spacing, the thing it was trying to feel like versus the thing it actually feels like. That gap is the growth. It's just not the kind of growth you can perceive overnight.

## So what is visual craft? 

If I strip away the principles and the process and the rationale-chasing, here's what I think visual craft really is: it's storytelling.

Every visual decision is a narrative choice. The whole discipline is just an elaborate way of asking: _what am I trying to make someone understand and feel, and does every choice on the screen serve that?_

The designers whose work stops me aren't the ones with the best technique or most flair. They're the ones whose interfaces tell a coherent story — where nothing on the screen is arguing with what the thing is trying to say. That's the craft. Not "does this look good," but "does this tell the truth about what it's for."

Get good at that, and the rest follows.
