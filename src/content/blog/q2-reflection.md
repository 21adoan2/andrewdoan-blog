# What Happens When the Safety Net Is Gone

There's a version of growth that happens gradually, in small increments, through feedback cycles and career ladders and mentorship. And then there's the version where your manager goes on leave, the projects land in your lap, and you realize pretty quickly whether or not you actually know what you're doing.

This past quarter was the second kind.

When my design lead stepped away unexpectedly, the team didn't fall apart — but it wobbled. My peer and I kept our heads down for a bit, doing the work, but quietly both of us were realizing the same thing: we didn't have a clear picture of our priorities, and nobody had handed us one. The bandwidth question was murky. The timeline question was murkier.

I could have kept quiet. A lot of people do. Instead, I organized a meeting with our team leads and just said it: we need help. We need to get aligned on what this quarter actually looks like given the circumstances. That conversation led to a real list of priorities, honest conversations about timeline risks, and a temporary check-in cadence with our design manager so nothing fell through the cracks.

It sounds simple when I write it. At the time, it took some nerve.

---

What followed was a few months of the most demanding — and most clarifying — design work I've done.

One project came with a one-week deadline for full hi-fidelity designs. The domain was complex, the problem space was sensitive, and the existing concepts were more directional than concrete. I couldn't loop in the broader design team for feedback the way I normally would. So I did what I could: leaned on my immediate team, posted regularly in shared channels, kept pulling in stakeholders to gut-check my thinking. The designs shipped. Then I spent the next week iterating based on expert feedback from people with deep domain knowledge I didn't have. Eventually, we reached real alignment. The team felt confident. I felt something close to proud.

The other project taught me something different.

When I picked it up, there was already a technical proposal in place — a document outlining the approach, including some design-adjacent assumptions. My instinct, as always, is to not take the problem statement at face value. So I read it carefully, then started asking questions from a user perspective.

What I found was a series of assumptions that seemed reasonable on the surface but didn't hold up under scrutiny. How users understand a piece of data, for example, turns out to vary a lot depending on who's looking at it and why. A single aggregate view isn't actually a good forecast for everyone — it collapses distinctions that matter. And a single-path model doesn't account for what happens when you're managing multiple assets simultaneously.

None of this broke the existing proposal. But it did reshape the design direction significantly. Through a lot of back-and-forth in shared channels and ongoing conversations with stakeholders, we landed somewhere better: a solution that shows the right information based on the context of what you're looking at. Not one-size-fits-all. Contextual.

That's the bottoms-up habit I keep coming back to. Assume the problem is more complex than it looks. Do the work before committing to the solution.

---

The other thing I found myself doing this quarter — almost without planning it — was building a culture of user research.

We didn't institute a formal process. No mandatory surveys, no submission forms, no quarterly checkboxes. What happened instead was that I kept broadcasting our team's interest in hearing from users, and other teams started including us. We ended up in sessions we wouldn't have been part of otherwise. Real conversations with real users about real problems.

I think this matters more than the alternative. Formal research programs can work, but they tend to work best when there's already an established feedback culture to plug into. When users don't have strong existing workflows or mental models — when *you're* the one helping define those — you need organic access to them. You need to meet them where they are, not where your process says they should be.

---

Late in the quarter, I was invited into a leads meeting. Not a regular thing for someone at my level. I heard a discussion about deprioritizing a usability audit that had come in from an external source.

The instinct in the room, as far as I could tell, was to take the feedback at face value and just execute on it.

I pushed back. Politely, but clearly.

Users are good at describing problems. They're much less reliable at designing solutions — not because they're not smart, but because that's not their job. When someone tells you a workflow is frustrating, that's real signal. When they tell you how to fix it, that's a hypothesis, not a prescription. Treating user-reported preferences as gospel without doing your own design thinking first is one of the more reliable ways to build something that technically addresses the feedback but doesn't actually solve anything.

If I hadn't been in that room, that point doesn't get made. No one's fault — it's just not the kind of thing that surfaces unless design is at the table.

---

I've been doing this job for a few years now, and this quarter is the first time I've felt like I could genuinely see the whole picture. Not just my piece of it, but how my decisions connect to the product, to the users, to the team's direction.

That feeling, I've realized, is mostly a function of ownership. When you're accountable for the outcome — not just the deliverable — you think differently. You ask better questions. You're less focused on executing the brief and more focused on whether the brief is right.

I didn't plan for this quarter to go the way it did. But I'm glad it did.
